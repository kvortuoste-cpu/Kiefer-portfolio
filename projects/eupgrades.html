Overview

Project Rebuild the eUpgrade cabin upgrade request experience from a 20-year-old third-party system to a modern in-house product
Role Lead designer: research, discovery, UX design, stakeholder management
Challenge Educating users on complex upgrade eligibility without surfacing the decision logic they actually needed
Outcome Launched 2026. Legacy system sunset. Operational cost reduced.

The Problem

eUpgrade is a cabin upgrade benefit earned by Aeroplan Elite members as part of their status. For nearly two decades, the experience to request an upgrade lived on an old platform that was dated, expensive, had no visual connection to the Air Canada brand, and had little context to how the upgrades actually worked. Power users managed, but newer Elite members get confused and question the value of the benefit. Air Canada had to rebuild the platform in-house and sunset the legacy platform to reduce operational cost and make eUpgrades feel like the premium benefit it is.

Discovery

I conducted research across different Elite status tiers, alongside interviewing the call centre team to understand what members knew, trusted, and couldn't figure out with the previous eUpgrade dashboard.

The clearest finding: members wanted transparency on their chances — a probability estimate, or at minimum an explanation of how Air Canada selects who gets upgraded.

The tension: We advocated for surfacing the upgrade decision logic, but ultimately the business was against it, because of legal and operational exposure they weren't willing to take on. The design had to solve the education problem without the one piece of information users wanted most.

Designing Around Constraint

Without transparency on the decision, the challenge became: how do you make someone feel informed when you can't give them the information that would actually inform them?

I landed on a set of contextual signals that reduce the information gap without exposing the underlying logic.

🖼️ Placeholder — insert diagram: transparency branching into View Seats, Seat Type Labelling, Important Notes, Confirmation Education

How the transparency problem branched into four contextual signals

View Seats

A seat map showing empty cabin seats, giving members a tangible reference point before committing credits.

Seat Type Labelling

Business class seats labelled as lie-flat or not, so members know exactly what they're requesting.

Important Notes

An educational section on the request page surfacing key information and what to expect after submitting.

Confirmation Education

Differentiated next steps for confirmed vs. waitlisted requests, addressing the moment of maximum confusion at exactly the right time.

Validation

We conducted two tests to validate our designs. The first was an A/B test to see if users preferred an AI-generated interface or a human-made interface. The second was a usability test to check that both power users and new users could easily navigate through the flow.

The AI Interface Test

Our senior director generated a candidate design using Figma Make. Rather than defaulting to seniority, we ran light user testing. Users preferred the human-designed interface — the layout was more familiar and surfaced more decision-relevant information. For this audience, familiarity and information density beat novelty.

The Usability Test

I tested the happy path, but also some edge cases where users had to find information to complete a task, to check if our contextual signals hit the mark.

The AI Pilot

The project became a pilot for AI-accelerated delivery, primarily targeting development and QA with AI tools and an automated testing framework. Designers weren't included in the pilot's original scope — we were asked to deliver designs before discovery was complete.

Pressurized short sprints, frameworks failing, tool upskilling, and expecting designs to be ready from the beginning pushed the team behind to the point where the original timeline wasn't feasible. The project returned to the original way of working for the sake of delivering.

The clearest lesson: a delivery acceleration pilot without design at the table from day one isn't a successful pilot. Designers should have a voice in how AI tooling gets adopted — not just absorb the downstream effects.

Outcome

With the launch of the new eUpgrades, we were able to deliver an experience that was built in-house, on-brand, and positioned to sunset the legacy third-party system, saving operational costs. Metrics on the impact to the call centre are still incoming.

Reflection

I feel that sometimes designing under a constraint you disagree with can lead to more considered solutions. The decision not to surface upgrade logic was right for the business, but not so much for users. Navigating that gap shaped every pattern we built. If I were starting again, I'd push harder to get design inside the AI pilot structure from the beginning, not downstream of it.
